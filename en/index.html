Basics

What a peptide is — and what it is not

Peptides are ordinary biology, but "peptide" does not mean "safe". Understanding the basics is your first filter against bad marketing.

Definition

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, usually 2 to 50, joined by peptide bonds. Longer chains are proteins. Insulin and growth hormone are peptide hormones, so peptides are part of how a healthy body already works.

How they act

Most bind to a specific receptor on the cell surface and pass a signal inward. The effect therefore depends on the receptor, the dose, the duration of exposure, and how quickly the body breaks the molecule down.

Form and absorption

The digestive tract destroys much of a peptide, which is why many are given subcutaneously or nasally. Oral bioavailability is usually low. Delivery form is drug design, not an accident.

Not steroids

Peptides and steroids are different chemical classes with different risk profiles. Some peptides are hormone secretagogues, meaning they act on your hormonal system. "Natural" is not a synonym for "risk-free".

Terms worth knowing

  • Secretagogue — triggers your own hormone release.
  • Half-life — how fast the body clears the substance.
  • Reconstitution — dissolving a powder before use.
  • CoA — certificate of analysis for a specific batch.
  • "Research chemical" — a label that means "not a medicine, not approved for people".

The most important distinction

Research interest is not the same as an approved medicine. For many popular peptides there are animal studies, lab models, or small trials — but no data on long-term safety in humans. Absence of evidence is not evidence of safety.

The peptides people ask about most

Each profile lists what it is studied for, its status in North Macedonia, and the main risk. This is orientation, not a recommendation and not an invitation to use anything.

BPC-157

Unapproved drug

Pentadecapeptide · healing

Derived from a gastric juice fragment. Studied in animals for tendons, muscle and the gut lining.

Risk: almost no controlled human trials, and no approval from any EU or US regulator.

TB-500

Unapproved drug

Thymosin β4 fragment · healing

Studied for cell migration and tissue regeneration after injury.

Risk: very limited human data; commonly sold as a "research chemical" with no medical supervision.

CJC-1295

Unapproved drug

GHRH analogue · hormones

An analogue of growth hormone releasing hormone, studied for raising GH and IGF-1 with a long half-life.

Risk: acts on the hormonal axis; without medical supervision it can disturb natural rhythms and affect blood sugar.

Ipamorelin

Unapproved drug

Ghrelin receptor agonist · hormones

A selective secretagogue studied for raising growth hormone with less impact on appetite and cortisol than earlier generations.

Risk: long-term safety in healthy people is unestablished; IGF-1 should be monitored by a doctor.

Semaglutide

Approved drug · prescription

GLP-1 receptor agonist · metabolism

This is a genuine approved medicine, dispensed by pharmacies on prescription (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus). It is used for type 2 diabetes and, for some products, for obesity treatment.

Risk: gastrointestinal side effects, pancreatitis risk, muscle loss without protein and training, and the need for gradual titration. Illegal "research" copies of the same molecule are dangerous — the molecule may be impure or wrongly dosed.

Semax and Selank

Unapproved drug

Synthetic ACTH fragments · cognitive

Nasal peptides developed in Russia and marketed for focus, stress and mood.

Risk: not registered in the EU; evidence for cognitive benefit in healthy people is weak and comes mainly from small studies.

Melanotan II

Unapproved · best avoided

α-MSH analogue · skin tanning

Sold for tanning faster without sun exposure.

Risk: European and US regulators have issued warnings over uncontrolled studies and reported serious reactions, including changes to moles and melanoma risk. This is one of the most dangerous "peptides" sold freely online.

GHK-Cu

Cosmetic ingredient

Copper peptide · cosmetics

Used in creams and serums, often with claims about collagen and healing.

Risk: cosmetic use is permitted, but claims that it "treats" anything are not. Cosmetic results are modest and not comparable to a medicine.

Collagen peptides

Food supplement

Hydrolysed collagen · food supplement

Legally available in pharmacies, supermarkets and online, sold for skin, joints and hair.

Risk: low risk for most healthy people; evidence of a measurable effect is modest and depends on overall diet rather than the product itself.

Epitalon

Unapproved drug

Tetrapeptide · longevity

Marketed with claims about telomerase and "biological ageing".

Risk: marketing runs ahead of the science; there are no human clinical trials, and telomerase biology is complex and potentially double-edged, including oncological risk.

Thymosin α-1

Approved in some countries

Immunomodulator · immunology

Used in certain countries (brand name Zadaxin) for specific indications under medical supervision.

Risk: not routinely available in Macedonian pharmacies; self-administering without an indication carries allergic reaction risk and unnecessary immune stimulation.

Framework

Legal status in North Macedonia

Most of the confusion comes from mixing up three different categories: a medicine, a food supplement, and a "research chemical".

  1. 01

    If it is a medicine, medicines law applies

    A substance claiming to treat, diagnose or prevent disease is a medicine. Medicines in North Macedonia are registered through MALMED, the Agency for Medicines and Medical Devices. An unregistered medicine may not be placed on the market or advertised.

  2. 02

    Prescription means prescription only

    Prescription medicines such as the GLP-1 products are not sold freely. Dispensing without a prescription is a violation, and a self-chosen dose is a health risk.

  3. 03

    Supplements and cosmetics are a different category

    Food supplements and cosmetics are not medicines. They are notified to the Food and Veterinary Agency (АХВ) and may not claim to cure disease. Collagen peptides belong here.

  4. 04

    Personal import is not automatic permission

    An order from a foreign website passes through customs and health controls. Unapproved or unregistered medicines can be seized, and responsibility stays with the person who ordered.

  5. 05

    "Research chemical" is not a legal category for you

    The "for research only, not for human use" label is how a seller distances itself. It does not mean the product is safe, tested, or permitted for personal use.

  6. 06

    Sport: doping lists

    Most hormonal and metabolic peptides are on the WADA prohibited list. If you compete, you risk sanctions and suspension, and testing methods keep improving.

How to check for yourself in five minutes

  1. Search for the active substance in the MALMED register.
  2. If it is not there, it is not an approved medicine in the country, however convincing it sounds.
  3. If it is a supplement, look for a notification and avoid products claiming to cure.
  4. If it is prescription-only, the conversation starts with a doctor, not a seller.

This is a general overview, not legal advice. For a specific situation, check with the competent authority or a lawyer specialising in health law.

Risk

Safety: red flags and habits

Most harm from peptides does not come from the molecule itself, but from impurities, wrong dosing, unsterile technique, and the absence of supervision.

Eight red flags

  • No certificate of analysis (CoA) for the specific batch.
  • A seller claiming the product "cures" or "replaces therapy".
  • Claims like "undetectable on doping tests".
  • Doses copied from a forum or a social media clip.
  • Powder to be mixed at home without sterile preparation.
  • No expiry date, batch number, or country of origin.
  • Payment with no invoice and no return address.
  • Peptides stored at room temperature and still sold as active.

If you do talk to a doctor

  1. Bring everything you take — supplements included, not just peptides.
  2. Ask for a baseline panel first: blood count, liver, kidney, hormones as needed.
  3. Introduce one substance at a time and log dose, date and symptoms.
  4. Do not stack hormonal secretagogues without IGF-1 monitoring.
  5. Plan a stop date and a follow-up visit.

Go to a doctor immediately if

Redness, swelling and heat at an injection site together with fever suggest infection. Symptoms such as severe headache, blurred vision, very high blood sugar, repeated vomiting or abdominal pain need urgent assessment. Do not wait for it to "pass", and do not add another dose.

Questions

Frequently asked

Can I legally buy peptides in Macedonia?

It depends on the category. A registered prescription medicine — only with a prescription, from a pharmacy. A notified food supplement or cosmetic — freely. A product sold as a "research chemical" has no legal category for personal use, and the shipment can be stopped at customs.

Are they safe?

There is no single answer. It depends on the substance, the dose, the purity, the route of administration and your own health. For most popular "healing" peptides there is weak or no controlled human data.

What does "research chemical, not for human use" mean?

It is a label that lets the seller disclaim responsibility and avoid medicines regulation. It does not mean the product is safer or tested. If the label says "not for human use", the product is not meant for you.

Why are peptides injected when pills exist?

They are broken down in the digestive tract, so oral absorption is poor. That is why many products are made for subcutaneous or nasal use. That advantage carries a risk: sterility, technique, and the possibility of a local reaction.

Are they detected on doping tests?

Many are on the WADA prohibited list and laboratory methods exist to detect them. Claims that something is "invisible" to testing are inaccurate and usually appear exactly when someone wants to sell.

How do I know a batch is clean?

A CoA (certificate of analysis) for that specific batch, with HPLC for purity, mass spectrometry for identity, and where relevant sterility and endotoxin testing. A certificate for one batch does not cover the next one.

Where do I report a side effect or a suspicious product?

For medicines — MALMED and your doctor. For supplements, food and cosmetics — АХВ. For suspicious online medicine sales, reporting to the competent authorities is a legitimate step, not an overreaction.
